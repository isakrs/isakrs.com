What makes a
good data platform?

Teams can find the data they need, understand what it means, use it safely, and recover when something goes wrong.

By Isak Rathe Støre · 11 November 2026 · About 7 minutes

A platform earns its place when it helps people ship something useful: an analysis they trust, a product feature, a forecast, or a reliable export to another system. I would judge it by the path from a producer’s data to that outcome.

This is my view of the engineering principles behind that path. The examples are conceptual, rather than a description of any employer’s production architecture.

01 / START WITH THE USE

Follow one event all the way through.

Imagine a payment event. A product team creates it, a pipeline receives it, transformations give it a useful shape, and a consumer uses the result. The end might be a dashboard, a model, or a product team receiving an approved dataset.

Each handoff needs an answer to three questions: what does this data mean, who is responsible for it, and what can the next person rely on? A diagram that stops at “the data is in storage” leaves most of the useful work unexplained.

02 / MAKE THE NORMAL PATH EASY

Offer a small set of dependable services.

I want teams to spend their time on their domain, rather than relearning how to create a job, request access, or publish a dataset. A good platform makes common work repeatable through versioned templates, configuration, and clear defaults.

The service should say what it accepts, what it produces, and what the team still owns. “Publish a dataset” is a useful promise. “Here is a workspace; good luck” leaves every team to build its own platform.

Standardisation also needs an escape route. An unusual workload may need custom code or different compute. Make that exception explicit, with an owner and a maintenance plan, rather than letting it become a hidden dependency.

03 / MAKE TRUST INSPECTABLE

A green job is only the beginning.

A pipeline can finish successfully while delivering duplicate events, stale data, or the wrong business definition. Trust needs evidence at the point where somebody consumes the result.

For the payment example, I would ask whether the event identifier is unique, the amount and currency have defined meanings, expected records arrived, and corrections are represented deliberately. These are different checks from whether the compute process exited successfully.

dbt data tests, for example, express assertions about data such as uniqueness, non-null values, and relationships. They are useful building blocks; a passing set of assertions only proves the conditions that were actually checked.

Make freshness, ownership, lineage, and known limitations easy to find next to the output. Access should have an explicit purpose and a manageable lifecycle. A dataset becoming available inside the platform should not automatically make it available to every downstream system.

04 / DESIGN FOR TIME AND FAILURE

The arrows need more than a direction.

“Data moves from A to B” hides important behaviour. Does the job append records, update existing ones, or replace the whole table? What happens after a missed day? What happens when an old event arrives twice?

Recovery should be a normal operation. Keep the inputs needed for the promised recovery window, record progress, and design retries to avoid applying the same logical change twice. Where source retention, privacy, or deletion rules prevent replay, document the boundary instead of promising unlimited recovery.

A practical runbook should explain how to identify the affected range, correct the cause, reprocess that range, validate the output, and tell consumers when it is trustworthy again. Delta Lake’s batch documentation describes mechanisms such as idempotent writes and historical reads. Those mechanisms still need an end-to-end recovery design, including retention and downstream effects.

05 / KEEP IT USEFUL TO OPERATE

Measure the promises people depend on.

I would rather see a small set of meaningful service measures than a wall of green infrastructure charts. How long does it take a team to publish its first useful dataset? Are consumers getting fresh enough data? Can an owner explain a failed delivery and recover it? Can costs be attributed to a workload?

Set targets from the use case. A daily report and an interactive feature need different delivery guarantees. Faster processing has little value when the source arrives once a day; a cheap daily batch is no bargain when the product requires timely updates.

Maintenance belongs in the product too: remove unused datasets, retire duplicate paths, keep dependencies understandable, and make ownership survive people changing teams.

The platform should become easier to use as it grows. That takes deliberate work on defaults, documentation, and the paths you choose to retire.

A PRACTICAL REVIEW

Try it with one real consumer.

Before adding another service or drawing another architecture diagram, walk one delivery through these questions:

  1. Use: What decision or product behaviour does this output enable?
  2. Meaning: Are identifiers, timestamps, updates, and business definitions explicit?
  3. Ownership: Who answers when the source changes or the output breaks?
  4. Trust: Can a consumer see freshness, quality checks, and limitations?
  5. Recovery: Can we correct a bad range and verify the result?
  6. Cost: Can we explain the operating cost and retire the output when it is no longer useful?

Clear answers are a better starting point than a longer list of tools. Build the smallest dependable path, learn from the people using it, and improve the parts that slow them down.
